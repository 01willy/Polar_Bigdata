\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Climate input, validation design and the in-region versus out-of-region contrast}
Replacing the 2011--2024 mean climate input by the input of the observation year reduced the spatial-block RMSE of the recalibrated Stefan model in the Lena Delta ($\Delta$RMSE \ndforcLePo{} cm) and that of the physics-residual model in the Lena Delta (\ndforcLeRo{} cm) and Canada (\ndforcCaRo{} cm), whereas in Alaska, where half of the labels come from one year, the change was not resolved (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}a). Under spatial-block cross-validation with this input, the physics-residual model reduced the RMSE of the recalibrated Stefan model from \nrmseLeblkPo{} to \nrmseLeblkRo{} cm in the Lena Delta ($\Delta$RMSE \ndrefLeblkRo{} cm) and from \nrmseCablkPo{} to \nrmseCablkRo{} cm in Canada (\ndrefCablkRo{} cm), whereas in Alaska their RMSEs were \nrmseAkblkPo{} and \nrmseAkblkRo{} cm (\ndrefAkblkRo{} cm; Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}b). The predictions of the recalibrated Stefan model span a narrow range in every region, because one coefficient per region distinguishes sites only through their TDD, whereas the predictions of the physics-residual model extend along the 1:1 line in the Lena Delta and Canada (Supplementary Fig.~S3). Direct ML, which learns the whole response from the labels, had the lowest site-year RMSE when all labels of a region were used (Alaska \nrmseAkblkDz{} versus \nrmseAkblkRo, Lena Delta \nrmseLeblkDz{} versus \nrmseLeblkRo{} and Canada \nrmseCablkDz{} versus \nrmseCablkRo{} cm for the physics-residual model). When the same predictions were scored on 1 km cell-year means, the unit of the maps, this advantage disappeared in Alaska (\ncyAkblkDz{} versus \ncyAkblkRo{} cm) and narrowed elsewhere (Supplementary Table~S5), which suggests that part of it comes from fitting differences between probe points inside a cell. The difference between direct ML and the recalibrated Stefan model was resolved only in Canada (\ndrefCablkDz{} cm).

The ranking of the methods depended on the validation design (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}b, Supplementary Table~S4). In random cross-validation, in which test sites lie next to training sites, direct ML had its lowest error (\nrmseAkrandDz{} cm in Alaska); spatial-block cross-validation raised its error by \noptimAkDz, \noptimLeDz{} and \noptimCaDz{} cm in Alaska, the Lena Delta and Canada, and the year and block-by-year designs raised it further. When an entire region was withheld, the direct-ML error in the Lena Delta rose to \nrmseLeregDz{} cm, against \nrmseLeregPz{} cm for the Stefan model with the coefficient of the other regions ($\Delta$RMSE \ndrefLeregDz{} cm), whereas the physics-residual model stayed within \ndrefLeregRoabs{} cm of the Stefan model there and improved on it in Canada (\ndrefCaregRo{} cm; Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}c). As test sites move away from training sites, the errors of the three methods change differently: the one-parameter Stefan model changes little with the design; direct ML is the most accurate where test and training sites interleave and the least accurate under region hold-out in the Lena Delta; and the physics-residual model keeps most of the in-region gain while its region hold-out error stays close to that of its anchor. Because maps are needed where there are no observations, spatial-block cross-validation is the main design below and the region hold-out the test of transferability.

The Kudryavtsev model, tested as an alternative anchor, was less accurate than the recalibrated Stefan model under spatial-block validation in Alaska (\nkuAkblk{} versus \nrmseAkblkPo{} cm) and the Lena Delta (\nkuLeblk{} versus \nrmseLeblkPo{} cm), more accurate in Canada (\nkuCablk{} versus \nrmseCablkPo{} cm), and less accurate under the region hold-out (\nrmseAkregKu{}, \nrmseLeregKu{} and \nrmseCaregKu{} cm against \nrmseAkregPz{}, \nrmseLeregPz{} and \nrmseCaregPz{} cm); the Stefan model was therefore kept (Supplementary Table~S4).

\subsection{Conditions of the physics-residual gain}
The gain of the residual learner depended on the available covariates (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}a). With climate covariates only, the residual model did not improve on the Stefan model in Alaska (\ndfeatAkFclim{} cm) or Canada (\ndfeatCaFclim{} cm); adding terrain and soil covariates produced the gains reported above in the Lena Delta and Canada, but not in Alaska. MODIS vegetation and snow timing or high-resolution soil and land-cover classes changed the result by less than 0.5 cm in the Lena Delta and Canada, and the latter added a small gain in Alaska (\ndfeatAkFhires{} cm). Across regional terciles of eight covariates, the gain occurred in most conditions of the Lena Delta (\ngcLesig{} of \ngcLecells{} terciles with intervals excluding zero) and Canada (\ngcCasig{} of \ngcCacells) and increased with the error of the Stefan model (correlation across terciles \ngcLecorr{} in the Lena Delta and \ngcCacorr{} in Canada; Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}b), reaching \ndtddLewarm{} cm in the warmest TDD tercile of the Lena Delta. In Alaska no tercile changed by more than \ngcAkmaxabs{} cm, so the absence of a gain was not confined to particular conditions (see Discussion). Terrain variables accounted for \nshapLeResTerrain\% of the mean absolute SHAP value of the residual model in the Lena Delta and soil variables for \nshapCaResSoil\% in Canada (Supplementary Fig.~S4). Of the structures that place the physics elsewhere, the pseudo-label model was close to the Stefan model (Lena Delta \ndrefLeblkDo{} cm) and the physics-input model was close to direct ML. Replacing CatBoost by four other learners did not change the sign of the gain in the Lena Delta and Canada (Supplementary Fig.~S2).

\subsection{Number of labels within a region and transfer to a new region}
The number of local observations that the learner needs was tested within regions and in transfer. With 25 training labels drawn from the site-years of a region, the physics-residual model was at least as accurate as a Stefan model recalibrated on the same labels (Alaska \nllAkRotwfive, Lena Delta \nllLeRotwfive{} and Canada \nllCaRotwfive{} cm), and with 100 labels its gain was resolved in all three regions (\nllAkRohundred, \nllLeRohundred{} and \nllCaRohundred{} cm; Supplementary Fig.~S5). Direct ML trained on 25 labels had a higher site-year RMSE than the Stefan model in Alaska (\nllAkDztwfive{} cm) and Canada (\nllCaDztwfive{} cm) and overtook the physics-residual model only with several hundred labels in Alaska and the Lena Delta. With thousands of labels direct ML had the lower error, whereas the physics-residual model reached almost the same accuracy with a fraction of the labels and was at least as accurate as the recalibrated Stefan model at every label number.

In the transfer experiment, each region was predicted by models trained on the other two regions and $n$ labels of the target region (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}c; $n \leq 500$, drawn from the training folds of the target region). Without target labels, direct ML reached \ntlrmseLeDszero{} cm in the Lena Delta, against \ntlrmseLePzzero{} cm for the source-coefficient Stefan model and \ntlrmseLeRszero{} cm for the physics-residual model; 25 target labels reduced the physics-residual error to \ntlrmseLeRstwfive{} cm and the direct-ML error to \ntlrmseLeDstwfive{} cm. In Alaska the physics-residual model gave the lowest error at every $n$ (\ntlrmseAkRszero{} cm without target labels). In Canada, direct ML trained on the source regions was the most accurate (\ntlrmseCaDszero{} cm). Recalibrating the Stefan coefficient on 25 Canadian labels increased its error from \ntlrmseCaPzzero{} to \ntlrmseCaPntwfive{} cm, and the error of the physics-residual model, which uses this anchor, increased from \ntlrmseCaRszero{} to \ntlrmseCaRstwfive{} cm. We attribute this to the difference in thaw depth between the two Canadian data sources (mean \nsrcCaABoVEObs{} cm at the probe transects and \nsrcCaCALMObs{} cm at the CALM grids), which one coefficient fitted to 25 labels cannot represent.

\subsection{Comparison with published products on the same sites and years}
The published products were evaluated on the same site-years as our models. On the \nccLeN{} common site-years of the Lena Delta (2010--2020; site-years at which Wei et al., Liu et al. and ESA CCI v4 all provide a value for the observation year), the physics-residual model reached an RMSE of \nccLeRoall{} cm, against \nccLeCcifiveall{} cm for ESA CCI v5, \nccLeAaltoall{} cm for Aalto et al., \nccLeCcifourall{} cm for ESA CCI v4, \nccLeLiuall{} cm for Liu et al. and \nccLeWeiall{} cm for Wei et al. (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}a, Supplementary Table~S6). More than 25 km from the training sites of Wei et al., the paired differences in the Lena Delta were \ndprodLeweiRomask{} cm relative to Wei et al. and \ndprodLeccifiveRomask{} cm relative to ESA CCI v5 (\nnprodLeweimask{} and \nnprodLeccifivemask{} site-years). In Alaska (\nccAkRoall{} versus \nccAkWeiall{} and \nccAkLiuall{} cm for Wei et al. and Liu et al.) and Canada (\nccCaRoall{} versus \nccCaWeiall{} and \nccCaLiuall{} cm) the model and the two ML products had similar errors.

In the Lena Delta the two ML products predicted between about 40 and 70 cm at most sites regardless of the observed ALT (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}b): the correlation between observed and predicted values was \nrLeWei{} for Wei et al. and \nrLeLiu{} for Liu et al., against \nrLeRo{} for the physics-residual model, and their mean bias was \nbiasLeAllWei{} and \nbiasLeAllLiu{} cm, against \nbiasLeAllRo{} cm, with the largest overestimation at the thin active layers of the northern delta (\nbiasLeNorthWei{} and \nbiasLeNorthLiu{} cm north of 72$^\circ$N). The error distributions (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}c) separate this systematic bias from the spread: in the Lena Delta the errors of the two ML products are shifted by about 20 cm with a spread similar to that of our model, whereas in Alaska and Canada all series are centred near zero and differ mainly in spread. The regional contrast corresponds to the location of the training sites of the products. Alaska label sites lie at a median distance of \ndistweiAkmed{} km from the training sites of Wei et al., and Liu et al. trained their model on an earlier release of the Alaska and Canada observations used here, so their values at these sites are not independent of our labels; the Lena Delta sites lie \ndistweiLemed{} km (median) from the training sites of Wei et al. (Supplementary Fig.~S6). A model trained without any Lena Delta label was already more accurate there than Wei et al. on the same site-years (\ntlvprmseLeRsWeizero{} versus \ntlvprmseLeWeizero{} cm; $\Delta$RMSE \ntlvpLeRsWeizero{} cm), so that the advantage in this region does not depend on local labels alone. Year by year, the physics-residual model had a lower error than Wei et al. in all \nywLen{} Lena Delta years with at least 30 site-years and in \nywAkRoWeiwin{} of \nywAkRoWein{} Alaska years (Supplementary Fig.~S7).

Maps of the year with the most observed cells in each region (2013 in Alaska and Canada, 2016 in the Lena Delta) show these differences (Fig.~\ref{fig:prodmaps}). At the \nfeAkN{} Alaska cells, the RMSE of the three maps was similar (\nfeAkOof{}, \nfeAkWei{} and \nfeAkLiu{} cm). The correlation with the observations was lower for our model (\nfeAkROof) than for the products (\nfeAkRWei{} and \nfeAkRLiu). Of these cells, \nfeAkNNear{} lie within 25 km of a training site of Wei et al.; at the \nfeAkNFar{} cells beyond that distance the RMSE of the three maps was \nfeAkFarOof{}, \nfeAkFarWei{} and \nfeAkFarLiu{} cm and the correlation \nfeAkFarROof{}, \nfeAkFarRWei{} and \nfeAkFarRLiu. Above 600 m between 68 and 70$^\circ$N, the products are deeper than our map by \nmapAkFootMtnWei{} and \nmapAkFootMtnLiu{} cm. In the Lena Delta (\nfeLeN{} cells observed in 2016) both products were deeper than the observations (mean bias \nfeLeBiasWei{} and \nfeLeBiasLiu{} cm; RMSE \nfeLeWei{} and \nfeLeLiu{} cm against \nfeLeOof{} cm). The residual learner can also be anchored to a product (equation~(\ref{eq:prod})): in the Lena Delta the Stefan-anchored model was more accurate than the model anchored to the Wei et al. product (\npadLeRoRp{} cm; Supplementary Fig.~S8), and in Alaska and Canada the two anchors were not distinguishable.

\subsection{Year-labelled 1 km ALT maps, 2011--2024}
The physics-residual models fitted to all site-years of each region were applied with the ERA5-Land climate of each year from 2011 to 2024 (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}) and cover \nncellmapAk{} cells in Alaska, \nncellmapLe{} in the Lena Delta and \nncellmapCa{} in Canada (NW). Relative to the 2011--2024 mean, the regional mean anomaly ranged from \nanminAk{} to \nanmaxAk{} cm in Alaska, from \nanminLe{} to \nanmaxLe{} cm in the Lena Delta and from \nanminCa{} to \nanmaxCa{} cm in Canada (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}d). These anomalies are the response of the model to the climate of each year. The regional mean anomaly correlates with the regional mean TDD to 1 September at \ntddanomAk{}, \ntddanomLe{} and \ntddanomCa{} in Alaska, the Lena Delta and Canada (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}d); the deepest regional thaw occurred in the warmest thaw seasons (\ntddmaxAk{}, \ntddmaxLe{} and \ntddmaxCa) and the shallowest in the coldest (\ntddminAk, \ntddminLe{} and \ntddminCa). In Alaska the anomaly of a warm year is largest in the interior lowlands and smallest on the North Slope (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}f). Because the set of observed sites changes between campaigns, yearly observed means cannot be compared directly with the mapped anomalies; Supplementary Fig.~S9 compares mapped and observed series at long-record sites instead. The linear trend of the yearly maps averaged \ntrendAk{}, \ntrendLe{} and \ntrendCa{} cm per decade over the Alaska, Lena Delta and Canada cells, with positive values in \ntrendposAk\%, \ntrendposLe\% and \ntrendposCa\% of the cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}e); it is the modelled response to the ERA5-Land climate of 2011--2024, not an observed trend. The area of applicability covers \naoaAk\%, \naoaLe\% and \naoaCa\% of the Alaska, Lena Delta and Canada cells; cells outside it lie mainly in mountains and at the southern margin of the permafrost zone (Supplementary Fig.~S10). The half-width of the 90\% prediction interval is \nhwAk{}, \nhwLe{} and \nhwCa{} cm, and the dissimilarity $d_k$ of each cell, mapped in Supplementary Fig.~S10, indicates the uncertainty of individual cells. Supplementary Fig.~S11 shows the residual correction.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The central result is that where the physical knowledge enters the model decides what the model is good at. In the physics-residual formulation (equation~(\ref{eq:residual})) the Stefan term carries the response to the thaw-season climate of each year in a bounded form, and the learner corrects the departures from one regional coefficient that soils and terrain impose. This division of labour explains the main patterns of the results. The gain over the Stefan model appeared only when terrain and soil covariates were available, because those are the variables that set the effective coefficient; it was largest where the Stefan model erred most, because that is where a correction has something to correct; and the attribution of the residual learner pointed to terrain in the Lena Delta and to soil organic carbon in Canada. Direct ML, which must learn the climate response from the labels, reached lower point errors when thousands of labels of a region were available, but with 25 labels it was less accurate than the Stefan model in Alaska and Canada and, when the Lena Delta was withheld, its error exceeded that of the Stefan model by \ndrefLeregDzabs{} cm. The physics-residual model gave up at most \ndrefRoDzblkmax{} cm of the in-region accuracy of direct ML and kept the transferability of its anchor, which matters where labels are scarce.

Alaska is the region in which the correction added nothing, and the reason is informative. With the year-specific input the Stefan term itself absorbs the between-year and between-campaign differences that a learner would otherwise recover from covariates, and the remaining error is the spread between probe points within 1 km cells (pooled standard deviation \ncyWithinSdAk{} cm), which no 1 km covariate resolves. The uniform absence of a gain across terciles (Fig.~\ref{fig:conditions}b) and the same result on cell-year means (Supplementary Table~S7) point to this explanation rather than to a shortcoming of the learner. A learner should therefore be added where the regional coefficient misrepresents the sites, which the spatial-block comparison with the recalibrated Stefan model detects, and not by default.

The comparison with the published products on the same sites and years reveals a pattern that their own validations cannot show. Where their training sites are close to our label sites (Alaska, and Canada for Liu et al., whose training data include the earlier release of the same field observations), the ML products were as accurate as our model, and the comparison there is not independent. Where they have no nearby training sites (the Lena Delta, median distance \ndistweiLemed{} km), they assigned values between about 40 and 70 cm regardless of the observed ALT, overestimated the thin active layers of the northern delta by about 20 cm, and were less accurate than a physics-residual model trained without any Lena Delta label. This is the behaviour of direct ML under region hold-out, reproduced by products whose predictors are yearly but whose climate response is learned from sites elsewhere; the Stefan anchor prevents the same failure because the response to the TDD of the stated year is carried by a term that does not depend on the training sites. The process-based ESA CCI product, which is not trained on ALT observations, was less accurate than our model in all three regions but, unlike the ML products, was not systematically biased in the Lena Delta (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}c), which is consistent with its physical basis.

These results extend previous comparisons in three directions. Gautam et al.\cite{Gautam2025} found at 68 Alaska CALM sites of one year that a random forest lost most of its accuracy on held-out sites whereas the Stefan model retained it; we confirm this ordering over site-years of 2010--2024 in three regions and under region hold-out, and show that the two approaches are complementary, because a learner that corrects the Stefan model retains its transferability. Studies of physics-guided learning in lakes and rivers reported that process knowledge helps mainly under data scarcity and extrapolation\cite{Read2019,Jia2021,Willard2022}; the label-number and region hold-out experiments give this statement a quantitative form for ALT. The finding that random cross-validation underestimates the spatial-block error of direct ML by \noptimLeDz--\noptimCaDz{} cm agrees with the literature on spatial validation\cite{Roberts2017,Ploton2020,MeyerPebesma2022}; errors from random or site-based splits therefore do not indicate the accuracy of a product in a region without training observations.

The yearly maps turn these results into a product with stated conditions of use. Each map is driven by the ERA5-Land climate of one year with the TDD accumulated to 1~September, so that it can be compared with observations or products of the same year, and it carries an area of applicability and a prediction interval. The regional mean anomalies of the maps are the modelled response to the thaw-season climate (correlation with the regional TDD \ntddanomAk{}--\ntddanomCa{}); they can be checked against observations only where the same sites are measured year after year, as at the long-record sites of Supplementary Fig.~S10, because elsewhere the set of observed cells changes between campaigns, and the linear trends of the maps describe the climate of 2011--2024 as transmitted by the model, not an observed trend. For a region not studied here, the label-number results indicate what to expect rather than a fixed procedure. Without local observations, the Stefan model with a coefficient from other regions, or the physics-residual model trained on other regions, is the safer starting point. Local labels are expected to help once they cover the local range of soils and terrain: 25 Lena Delta labels reduced the error of the physics-residual model from \ntlrmseLeRszero{} to \ntlrmseLeRstwfive{} cm, whereas 25 Canadian labels increased the error of the recalibrated Stefan model from \ntlrmseCaPzzero{} to \ntlrmseCaPntwfive{} cm, which we attribute to the wide range of thaw depths among the Canadian sites. The spatial-block comparison within the new region and the area of applicability of the resulting map are the checks that distinguish these cases.

The study has limitations that bound these conclusions. Half of the Alaska labels come from 2014, which prevented a resolved estimate of the effect of the year-specific input there, and the Lena Delta labels come from one data set in \nnblkLe{} blocks, which widens the intervals. The labels combine probe transects and CALM grid means from three programmes, and the model error differs between them (Supplementary Table~S8), most clearly at the \nsrcCaCALMN{} Canadian CALM site-years, whose mean ALT of \nsrcCaCALMObs{} cm is underestimated by \nsrcCaCALMBias{} cm; differences between measurement protocols and site selection are therefore part of the reported errors. The labels are observation sites rather than independent 1~km cells, which the spatial-block design and the cell-year scores account for. The climate input is monthly and the soil covariates are static; the Kudryavtsev model, which includes snow and moisture terms, was more accurate than the recalibrated Stefan model in Canada (\nkuCablk{} versus \nrmseCablkPo{} cm) but less accurate in Alaska (\nkuAkblk{} versus \nrmseAkblkPo{} cm), and the residual learner recovered most of the Canadian difference, which suggests that a moisture-aware anchor could reduce it further. Active layers thicker than about 3~m lie outside the training range, and the comparison of maps with observations in one year rests on few cells in the Lena Delta and Canada. Daily climate inputs, remotely sensed snow and soil moisture, and labels from a fourth region are the next steps; because every number here is tied to a stated site, year and validation design, the protocol can be applied to new products and regions as they appear, and the maps extended year by year.

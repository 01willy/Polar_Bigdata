The active layer is the near-surface ground above permafrost that thaws each summer and refreezes each winter. Its thickness (ALT) sets the depth of seasonal thaw and controls ground stability, near-surface hydrology and the amount of soil organic carbon exposed to decomposition\cite{Hjort2018,Schuur2015,Biskaborn2019}. ALT responds to the thermal regime of individual years, so that interannual variability is an intrinsic part of the signal recorded by long-term monitoring programmes such as the Circumpolar Active Layer Monitoring (CALM) network\cite{Nelson1997,Streletskiy2025}. Observations, however, are sparse and spatially clustered: most measurements originate from a small number of intensive field campaigns and long-term grids, and many sites are visited only once\cite{Moore2025,Veremeeva2025}.

Several gridded ALT products at about 1 km resolution now cover the northern permafrost region. Process-based estimates such as ESA CCI Permafrost solve the ground heat equation forced by remotely sensed land-surface temperature\cite{Westermann2024,Westermann2025}, whereas statistical and machine-learning (ML) products relate site observations to climate, terrain and soil predictors\cite{Aalto2018,Ran2022a,Liu2024,Wei2026}. Three limitations restrict their use for year-specific applications. First, most products are trained on multi-year mean predictors or on site-years pooled across many years, so that a product value is not tied to the climate of a stated year. Second, products are evaluated with different site sets and validation schemes; reported errors range, for example, from a random ten-fold root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 21.6 cm\cite{Liu2024} to a leave-one-site-out RMSE of 60.5 cm\cite{Wei2026}, and cannot be compared with each other. Third, random cross-validation of spatially clustered observations overstates the accuracy of ML models at new locations\cite{Roberts2017,Ploton2020,MeyerPebesma2022,Wadoux2021}, which obscures how a model would perform in a region without observations.

Physically based models offer a complementary route. The Stefan solution relates thaw depth to the thawing degree-days (TDD) of the season through a single coefficient that depends on soil thermal properties and moisture\cite{Stefan1891,Riseborough2008,Nelson1997}. It reproduces the seasonal progression of thaw and responds to the climate of each year, but a single coefficient per region leaves systematic residuals associated with soil organic matter and terrain. Physics-guided ML, in which a physical model carries the dominant response and a learner corrects what the physics omits, has improved accuracy and extrapolation in several environmental applications\cite{Willard2022,Read2019,Jia2021}, and in Alaska the Stefan model retained its accuracy on held-out sites better than a random forest\cite{Gautam2025}. It remains unclear how such a combination performs when the climate input is matched to the observation year, how it transfers to a region without labels, how many local labels a new region requires, and how it compares with published products in the same cells and years.

Here we address these questions with a cell-year design, in which each ALT observation is a 1 km cell in a stated year and each climate input is computed from ERA5-Land for that year and accumulated to the observation day\cite{munozsabater2021_era5_land}. Using \nncyAll{} cell-year observations from 2010--2024 in Alaska, the Lena Delta and north-western Canada, we compare the Stefan model, direct ML and a physics-residual workflow in which gradient boosting learns the residual of a recalibrated Stefan model. In contrast to previous ALT mapping studies, which mostly report random or site-based cross-validation on product-specific site sets\cite{Liu2024,Wei2026,Ran2022a}, all methods and products are scored here against the same observations and under the same validation designs. The study makes four contributions. (i) It removes the temporal mismatch between ALT labels and climate inputs and quantifies the effect of the year-specific input. (ii) It evaluates physics-residual learning against direct ML, physics-input ML and physics pseudo-labels under five validation designs, including the hold-out of an entire region. (iii) It quantifies sample efficiency and transferability with label-number experiments within and across regions and in external CALM regions. (iv) It compares models and six published products in the same cells and years, with masks for product training sites, and provides year-labelled 1 km maps for 2011--2024 with an area of applicability and prediction intervals.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Effect of the year-specific climate input}
The observations are unevenly distributed in time (Fig.~\ref{fig:data}b, Table~\ref{tab:data}). In Alaska, \ntopyrshareAk\% of the cell-years were observed in \ntopyrAk, whereas the Lena Delta labels are spread over \nnyrLe{} years. Most measurements were made in August (median day of year \ndoymedAk{} in Alaska, \ndoymedLe{} in the Lena Delta and \ndoymedCa{} in Canada), before the end of the thaw season, and the TDD accumulated to the observation day amounted to a median of \ntddfracAk, \ntddfracLe{} and \ntddfracCa{} of the full-season sum (equation~(\ref{eq:tddobs})).

Replacing the mean climate input by the year-specific input reduced the spatial-block RMSE of the recalibrated Stefan model in the Lena Delta ($\Delta$RMSE \ndforcLePo{} cm) and that of the physics-residual model in the Lena Delta (\ndforcLeRo{} cm) and Canada (\ndforcCaRo{} cm) (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}a). In Alaska the change was not resolved (Stefan \ndforcAkPo{} cm), consistent with the concentration of the labels in one year, for which the year-specific input adds little information beyond the spatial pattern. The year-specific input also ties each prediction to the climate of a stated year, which is the prerequisite for the yearly maps presented below.

\subsection{Accuracy under spatial, temporal and regional validation}
Model rankings depended on the validation design (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}b, Supplementary Table~S5). Random cross-validation yielded the lowest errors for direct ML (\nrmseAkrandDz{} cm in Alaska), and spatial-block cross-validation increased the direct-ML error by \noptimAkDz, \noptimLeDz{} and \noptimCaDz{} cm in Alaska, the Lena Delta and Canada. Year hold-out increased the direct-ML error in the Lena Delta from \nrmseLeblkDz{} to \nrmseLeyrDz{} cm, reflecting the spread of its labels over many years, and the block-by-year design produced the largest within-region errors for all methods fitted to labels of the region in all three regions. When an entire region was withheld, the direct-ML error in the Lena Delta rose to \nrmseLeregDz{} cm, compared with \nrmseLeregPz{} cm for the Stefan model with the coefficient of the other regions ($\Delta$RMSE \ndrefLeregDz{} cm). In the same test the physics-residual model remained close to the Stefan model (\ndrefLeregRo{} cm) and improved on it in Canada (\ndrefCaregRo{} cm). Spatial-block cross-validation is therefore used as the main design and region hold-out as the test of transferability.

\subsection{Gain of physics-residual learning and its controls}
Under spatial-block cross-validation with the year-specific input, the physics-residual model reduced the RMSE of the recalibrated Stefan model from \nrmseLeblkPo{} to \nrmseLeblkRo{} cm in the Lena Delta ($\Delta$RMSE \ndrefLeblkRo{} cm) and from \nrmseCablkPo{} to \nrmseCablkRo{} cm in Canada (\ndrefCablkRo{} cm), whereas both models reached about \nrmseAkblkRo{} cm in Alaska (\ndrefAkblkRo{} cm). The gain depended on the covariates available to the residual learner (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}c). With climate covariates only, the residual model did not improve on the Stefan model in Alaska (\ndfeatAkFclim{} cm) or Canada (\ndfeatCaFclim{} cm); adding terrain and soil covariates produced the gains above. MODIS vegetation and snow timing (2010--2024) or high-resolution soil and land-cover classes changed the result by less than 0.5 cm in the Lena Delta and Canada, and the latter added a small gain in Alaska (\ndfeatAkFhires{} cm).

The result depended on how the physical model entered the learner (Supplementary Fig.~S2). Augmenting the training set with Stefan pseudo-labels reproduced the Stefan model (Lena Delta \ndrefLeblkDo{} cm), and using the Stefan prediction as an input feature yielded results similar to direct ML. With all labels of a region, direct ML reached lower point errors than the physics-residual model (Alaska \nrmseAkblkDz{} versus \nrmseAkblkRo, Lena Delta \nrmseLeblkDz{} versus \nrmseLeblkRo{} and Canada \nrmseCablkDz{} versus \nrmseCablkRo{} cm), but its difference from the recalibrated Stefan model was resolved only in Canada (\ndrefCablkDz{} cm), and its error increased under region hold-out. Replacing CatBoost by random forest, a multilayer perceptron, TabM or FT-Transformer did not change the sign of the physics-residual gain in the Lena Delta and Canada.

Across regional terciles of eight covariates, the gain occurred in most conditions of the Lena Delta (\ngcLesig{} of \ngcLecells{} terciles with intervals excluding zero) and Canada (\ngcCasig{} of \ngcCacells) and increased with the error of the Stefan model; across terciles, the correlation between the Stefan error and the gain was \ngcLecorr{} in the Lena Delta and \ngcCacorr{} in Canada (Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy}d). In the warmest TDD tercile of the Lena Delta the gain reached \ndtddLewarm{} cm, whereas no tercile in Alaska changed by more than \ngcAkmaxabs{} cm. These patterns indicate that the residual learner corrects departures of the effective Stefan coefficient from its regional value that arise from soil and terrain. Consistent with this interpretation, terrain variables accounted for \nshapLeResTerrain\% of the mean absolute SHAP value of the residual model in the Lena Delta and soil variables for \nshapCaResSoil\% in Canada (Supplementary Fig.~S3).

\subsection{Sample efficiency and transfer to new regions}
The label-number experiments show how the methods behave when labels are scarce (Fig.~\ref{fig:labels}a). With 25 training labels drawn from the 2010--2024 cell-years of a region, the physics-residual model was at least as accurate as a Stefan model recalibrated on the same labels (Alaska \nllAkRotwfive, Lena Delta \nllLeRotwfive{} and Canada \nllCaRotwfive{} cm), and with 100 labels its gain was resolved in all three regions (\nllAkRohundred, \nllLeRohundred{} and \nllCaRohundred{} cm). Direct ML trained on 25 labels had a higher point error than the Stefan model in Alaska (\nllAkDztwfive{} cm) and Canada (\nllCaDztwfive{} cm). Physics-input ML and physics pseudo-labels followed the patterns of their full-data results (Supplementary Fig.~S4).

\paperfig{Fig4_labels}{1.0}{\textbf{Accuracy with few labels and in new regions.} \textbf{a}, $\Delta$RMSE between each method and a Stefan model recalibrated on the same $n$ training labels within each region (labels of 2010--2024, three draws); the band is the 95\% block-bootstrap interval of the physics-residual model. \textbf{b}, RMSE in each region for models trained on the other two regions plus $n$ labels from the target region. \textbf{c}, RMSE in the external CALM regions (observations of 2010--2024) against the number of labels from the same external region, on the \nftrows{} cell-years with values of Wei et al., Liu et al. and ESA CCI v5 (2010--2020); horizontal lines mark the products, of which Wei et al. and Liu et al. include these sites in their training data.}{fig:labels}

In the transfer experiment, each region was predicted by models trained on the other two regions and $n$ labels from the target region (Fig.~\ref{fig:labels}b). Without target labels, direct ML reached \ntlrmseLeDszero{} cm in the Lena Delta, compared with \ntlrmseLePzzero{} cm for the source-coefficient Stefan model and \ntlrmseLeRszero{} cm for the physics-residual model. Twenty-five target labels reduced the error of the physics-residual model to \ntlrmseLeRstwfive{} cm and that of direct ML to \ntlrmseLeDstwfive{} cm. In Alaska the physics-residual model gave the lowest error at every $n$ (\ntlrmseAkRszero{} cm without target labels). In Canada, direct ML trained on the source regions was the most accurate (\ntlrmseCaDszero{} cm); recalibrating the Stefan coefficient on 25 Canadian labels increased the error from \ntlrmseCaPzzero{} to \ntlrmseCaPntwfive{} cm, and the physics-residual model, which uses this anchor, followed (\ntlrmseCaRszero{} to \ntlrmseCaRstwfive{} cm), reflecting the wide range of thaw depths among the Canadian sites.

The same procedure was applied to eight external CALM regions with \nftScoredRows{} scored cell-years from 2010--2024 that were not used in model development (Fig.~\ref{fig:labels}c, Supplementary Fig.~S5). On the \nftrows{} cell-years of seven regions with values of Wei et al., Liu et al. and ESA CCI v5, the physics-residual model trained on the three study regions reached \nftAllRszero{} cm without local labels. Five labels from the same external region reduced the error to \nftAllRsfive{} cm and 25 labels to \nftAllRstwfive{} cm, whereas direct ML reached \nftAllDstwfive{} cm with 25 labels. With five labels the physics-residual model was more accurate than ESA CCI v5, which is not trained on ALT observations (\nftAllCcifivezero{} cm on the same \nftrows{} cell-years; $\Delta$RMSE \nftdRsCcifivefive{} cm on all \nftdCciRows{} cell-years with an ESA CCI v5 value). Wei et al. and Liu et al., which include these sites in their training data, reached \nftAllWeizero{} and \nftAllLiuzero{} cm. Most of the reduction came from recalibrating the Stefan coefficient; the residual learner did not reduce the error further at these sites (\nftdRsPnfive{} cm with five labels on all \nftScoredRows{} cell-years), where the active layers of West and Central Siberia and Svalbard exceed the range of the study regions. The pooled values combine different regional responses: with five labels the error decreased in West Siberia, Central Siberia, European Russia and Svalbard, but increased in Chukotka (\nftChRszero{} to \nftChRsfive{} cm) and Kamchatka (\nftKaRszero{} to \nftKaRsfive{} cm), where the error without local labels was below 10 cm, and in north-eastern Siberia (\nftNsRszero{} to \nftNsRsfive{} cm).

\subsection{Comparison with published products in the same cells and years}
On the common cell-years (\nyrscc), the physics-residual model reached \nccLeRoall{} cm in the Lena Delta, compared with \nccLeCcifiveall{} cm for ESA CCI v5, \nccLeAaltoall{} cm for Aalto et al., \nccLeCcifourall{} cm for ESA CCI v4, \nccLeLiuall{} cm for Liu et al. and \nccLeWeiall{} cm for Wei et al. (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}a, Supplementary Table~S6). Excluding cells within 25 km of product training sites, the paired differences were \ndprodLeweiRomask{} cm relative to Wei et al. and \ndprodLeccifiveRomask{} cm relative to ESA CCI v5. In Alaska (\nccAkRoall{} versus \nccAkWeiall{} and \nccAkLiuall{} cm for Wei et al. and Liu et al.) and Canada (\nccCaRoall{} versus \nccCaWeiall{} and \nccCaLiuall{} cm), the model and the two ML products performed on par.

\paperfig{Fig5_products}{1.0}{\textbf{Comparison with published products in the same cells and years.} \textbf{a}, RMSE of three methods (spatial-block out-of-fold predictions) and five products on the common cell-years (\nyrscc), for all cells and for cells more than 25 km from the training sites of Wei et al.; ESA CCI v5 and Aalto et al. are scored where they have a value; values beyond the axis are printed. \textbf{b}, RMSE per observation year on the same cell-years of each year, for years with at least 30 cell-years (Alaska \nyrsywAk; Lena Delta \nyrsywLe; Canada \nyrsywCa); Liu et al. end in 2020; bars give the number of cell-years per year.}{fig:products}

The regional contrast is consistent with the training data of the products. Alaska label cells lie at a median distance of \ndistweiAkmed{} km from the training sites of Wei et al., and Liu et al. were trained on an earlier version of the same Alaska and Canada field data, whereas the Lena Delta cells lie at a median distance of \ndistweiLemed{} km (Supplementary Fig.~S6). Both products overestimate the thin active layers of the northern delta, with mean biases of \nbiasLeNorthWei{} and \nbiasLeNorthLiu{} cm north of 72$^\circ$N. A model trained without any Lena Delta label was already more accurate there than Wei et al. on the same cell-years (\ntlvprmseLeRsWeizero{} versus \ntlvprmseLeWeizero{} cm; $\Delta$RMSE \ntlvpLeRsWeizero{} cm), indicating that the advantage in this region does not depend on local labels alone.

Maps of the same year show these differences in space (Fig.~\ref{fig:prodmaps}). For Alaska in 2014, the three maps agree at the observed cells (RMSE \nfeAkOof{} cm for the out-of-fold predictions of our model, \nfeAkWei{} cm for Wei et al. and \nfeAkLiu{} cm for Liu et al.), of which \nfeAkNearWei{} of \nfeAkN{} lie within 25 km of a training site of Wei et al., and the largest differences between maps occur in mountain terrain above 600 m, which lies largely outside the area of applicability and where the products are deeper than our map by \nmapAkFootMtnWei{} and \nmapAkFootMtnLiu{} cm between 68 and 70$^\circ$N. For the Lena Delta in 2016, where all \nfeLeN{} observed cells lie more than 25 km from these training sites, both products are deeper than the observations across the delta (mean bias \nfeLeBiasWei{} and \nfeLeBiasLiu{} cm; RMSE \nfeLeWei{} and \nfeLeLiu{} cm), whereas our model reaches an RMSE of \nfeLeOof{} cm.

Year by year, the physics-residual model had a lower error than both Wei et al. and the Stefan model in all \nywLen{} Lena Delta years with at least 30 cell-years (\nyrsywLe), in \nywAkRoWeiwin{} of \nywAkRoWein{} Alaska years against Wei et al. and \nywAkRoPowin{} of \nywAkRoPon{} against the Stefan model (\nyrsywAk), and in \nywCaRoPowin{} of \nywCaRoPon{} Canadian years against the Stefan model (Fig.~\ref{fig:products}b). At Alaska cells observed in several years, the correlation between observed and predicted year-to-year changes was \nrdeltaAkRo{} for the physics-residual model and \nrdeltaAkWei{} and \nrdeltaAkLiu{} for Wei et al. and Liu et al., whereas in the Lena Delta the products followed the interannual changes more closely (\nrdeltaLeWei{} and \nrdeltaLeLiu{} versus \nrdeltaLeRo).

The residual learner can also be applied with a product as the anchor (equation~(\ref{eq:prod})). With the Lena Delta labels, adding the residual learner to the recalibrated product of Wei et al. changed its error by \npadLeRpPcal{} cm, and the Stefan-anchored model remained more accurate than the product-anchored model (\npadLeRoRp{} cm; Supplementary Fig.~S7). In Alaska and Canada the two anchors were not distinguishable.

\subsection{Year-labelled 1 km ALT maps, 2011--2024}
The physics-residual models fitted to all cell-years of Alaska and the Lena Delta were applied with the ERA5-Land climate of each year from 2011 to 2024 (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}). The maps cover \nncellmapAk{} cells in Alaska and \nncellmapLe{} in the Lena Delta. Relative to the 2011--2024 mean, the regional mean anomaly ranged from \nanminAk{} cm in \nanminyrAk{} to \nanmaxAk{} cm in \nanmaxyrAk{} in Alaska and from \nanminLe{} cm in \nanminyrLe{} to \nanmaxLe{} cm in \nanmaxyrLe{} in the Lena Delta, with spatial patterns that differ between years (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}b). In the Lena Delta, the mean anomaly of the observed cells had the same sign as the predicted regional anomaly in \nanobssameLe{} of \nanobsnLe{} years with at least 50 cell-years (\nanobsyrsLe; $r$ = \nanobsrLe), and the correlation remained \nanobsrLeNoTwenty{} without 2020, when the observed anomaly of \nanobsLeTwentyN{} cell-years reached \nanobsLeTwentyObs{} cm. In Alaska, where the set of observed cells changes between campaigns, the agreement was weaker ($r$ = \nanobsrAk), and the predicted interannual range (\nanpredrangeAk{} cm) was smaller than that of the observed cell means (\nanobsrangeAk{} cm) (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}c). The area of applicability covers \naoaAk\% of the Alaska cells and \naoaLe\% of the Lena Delta cells; cells outside it lie mainly in mountains and at the southern margin of the permafrost zone (Supplementary Fig.~S8). The half-width of the 90\% prediction interval is \nhwAk{} and \nhwLe{} cm. The correction added by the residual learner is mostly within $\pm$10 cm (Supplementary Fig.~S9), and series at long-record cells are shown in Supplementary Fig.~S10.
